# Using the family tree

For anyone in the family who has been given an account. Nothing here assumes
you know anything about computers beyond opening a web page.

Whoever set this up will have sent you three things: a web address, a username
and a password. Keep this page open beside the site the first time.

## Signing in

Open the address you were given. You will see the tree, or a page inviting you
to look at it. Some of the family is visible to anyone who opens the page;
the rest appears once you sign in, which is the point of your account.

Along the top there are four or five words: **Tree**, **Import**, a language
name, **Admin**, and at the right **Sign in**. Click **Sign in**.

You are asked for a username and a password. Type the two you were sent and
press **Sign in**. Nothing else on that page is for you — there is a folded
section further down about an "emergency token", which is for whoever looks
after the machine on the day everything has gone wrong. Ignore it.

Two things that happen to everybody once:

- **"That username and password do not match."** — the password was almost
  certainly pasted with a space at the end, or the capital letters were
  mistyped. Try again slowly.
- **"Too many failed attempts. Wait a few minutes and try again."** — the site
  stops guessing after eight tries. It is not broken and you are not locked
  out; wait five minutes.

You stay signed in for twelve hours. After that, sign in again.

## Finding a person

Two ways.

**Through the tree.** Click **Tree** at the top. You get a drawing of the
family: each person is a box, lines join parents to children, and couples sit
side by side. Click any box and that person's page opens. If the tree is large,
it is easier to start from someone you know and walk outwards — every page has
a link reading **See this person in the tree**, which redraws it centred on
them.

**Through the list.** Click **Admin** at the top, then **Persons**. That is
every person in the archive, in alphabetical order, in pages of fifty. There is
a search box above the list: type part of a name and press Enter. Searching for
`kowal` finds Kowalski and Kowalczyk alike.

Names in this kind of archive repeat — there may well be eight people called
Maria. The list shows each person's years beside their name so you can tell
which is which, and where two are still indistinguishable, the last part of
their internal reference (`#3f81d0a6`) is shown too. You never need to type one
of those.

## What a person's page shows you

The page opens on **Record**: names, dates, places, and whatever else has
been written down. Above it are tabs, and a tab is there only when the
record holds something for it:

| Tab | What is behind it |
| --- | --- |
| **Record** | Who the person was: names, the main facts, notes. |
| **Family** | Parents, brothers and sisters, partners and children, each with how sure the link is; godparents and the other ties of rite, in dotted frames; anyone else the record is linked to. |
| **Kinship** | How anyone in the family is related to this person, and every relative they have. See [How are we related?](#how-are-we-related) |
| **Events** | The life in order: births, marriages, moves, deaths. |
| **Sources** | The registers, certificates and letters the facts come from. |
| **Media** | Photographs and scanned documents attached to this person. |
| **Profile** | What they were like: build, colouring, temperament, skills. Most records have little or nothing here, and that is normal. |

Nothing in this archive is quietly overwritten. Every saved change is kept
with the name of whoever made it: if you change a date and somebody
disagrees next month, the original is still there with your name against
it.

Some records show a line saying that something is being withheld. That is not
a fault. A living person's private details are shown only to people allowed to
see them, and the page says so rather than pretending the information does not
exist.

## How are we related?

Open a person's page and choose the **Kinship** tab. It answers two
questions.

**How is this person related to someone else?** Under *How are they
related?*, choose the other person in the list. The answer appears
straight away (if it does not, press **Show the link**):

- the word: *second cousin*, *great-aunt*, *wife of a nephew*, *godmother*;
- whose relative that is;
- the ancestors the two of them share, if they share any;
- the way from one to the other, a step at a time: *father*, *mother*,
  *daughter*… Every name on the way opens that person's page;
- the degree of kinship: the steps up to the shared ancestor, plus the
  steps back down.

When two people are related in several ways (a cousin who is also a
godmother, say), every way is listed, the closest first.

**Who are all this person's relatives?** Below, the tab lists them in
groups: partners, ancestors, descendants, brothers and sisters, uncles and
aunts, nephews and nieces, cousins, family by marriage, stepfamily,
fostering and guardianship, rite and custom, and people connected some other
way. A long group is folded: click its name to open it. **Show the link**
beside anyone gives you the way to them.

The tree says it too. Click a box in the tree and the card that opens says
what that person is to the person in the middle; the panel at the side has a
link reading **The link, step by step**.

Some words, explained:

- **Half-brother, half-sister**: one parent in common rather than two.
  Children the record lists in one family are brothers and sisters even
  when nobody knows who their parents were.
- **Once removed**: a generation apart. Your father's first cousin is your
  first cousin once removed, and so is your first cousin's child.
- **Adoptive**: said where it matters. An adopted child is a child of the
  family.
- **Stepfather, foster mother, guardian**: when the family's record says
  the child belongs to that household as a stepchild, a foster child or a
  ward.
- **A person you may not see**: somebody on the way whose details are
  private. The link is still shown; the name is not.
- If the records connect two people in no way that has a name, the tab
  shows how they are connected, fact by fact.

The words are in the language you chose. English and French have been read
by people who speak them; the other languages have not been yet.

## Godparents and the other ties of rite

A godmother, a *kum*, a milk sister, a namesake: many families keep ties
made by a ceremony or a custom rather than by birth. The archive records
them beside the family, never inside it. A godmother is never shown as a
mother, and these ties are drawn **dotted**.

On a person's page, the **Family** tab has a section *Rite and custom*. Each
tie sits in a dotted frame: what the other person is (*godmother*), the
rite (*Baptism*), the tradition (*Christian*), and the record's own words
when it gave any (*matka chrzestna*). In the tree, a dotted curved line
joins the two people when both are drawn, in a colour no family line uses,
and the legend at the top says what it is.

**To add one** (you need to be allowed to edit):

1. Open the person's page and click **Edit**.
2. In the **Family** tab of the editor, click **Edit links**.
3. Under **A new link**, choose the other person in **The other end**.
4. In **Rite or custom**, choose the tie. The list is grouped by
   tradition, and each choice says what *this* person is to the other:
   *Baptism: godparent* if this person is the godmother, *Baptism:
   godchild* if this person is the godchild.
5. Leave **What it is** empty to use the usual words, or type the record's
   own: *marraine*, *matka chrzestna*.
6. Press **Create the link**.

The tie then shows on both people's pages.

Twenty-seven ties are on the list, from ten traditions: Christian
(godparents at a baptism or a confirmation, sponsors of a marriage),
Orthodox (*kum*, *koumbaros*), Hispanic and Filipino (*padrino*,
*compadre*, *ninong*), Jewish (*sandek*, *kvatter*), Islamic (milk kinship,
*kafala*), South Asian (*rakhi*, the guru), East Asian (*gan qin*
godparents, sworn siblings, the name-giver, the *nakōdo*), African
(namesakes, initiation, the blood pact), Indigenous and Pacific (*hānai*,
*hunka*), and civil (civil godparenthood, guardianship). If yours is not
there, choose *None: a plain relation* and write the words yourself.

## A polygamous union

When a man had several wives at once (or a woman several husbands), the
archive does not read it as one big couple. It reads it as **one marriage
per wife**:

- the husband is married to each wife, and the wives are not married to
  one another: each is the others' **co-wife**;
- each marriage has its own dates and its own end;
- each child belongs to their mother's marriage, so the children of two
  wives are **half**-brothers and half-sisters.

On a wife's page, her union shows her husband and, below him, her
**co-wives**. On the husband's page, each marriage is a block of its own,
with a double line down its side. In the tree, the bracket that joins a
husband and a wife is drawn in long and short strokes, and its knot has
three rings instead of two.

**To record one** (you need to be allowed to edit):

1. Open the husband's page and click **Edit**.
2. In the **Family** tab, on the union, set the type to *polygamous* and add
   each wife as a partner. Press **Save**.
3. The union now asks two more things. In **At the centre of the union**,
   choose the husband. For each child, in **Other parent**, choose the
   child's mother; leave it unset if nobody knows.
4. Press **Save**.

If the family was recorded as one family per marriage (that is how GEDCOM
files arrive), set the type of each of those unions to *polygamous*. The
archive reads them the same way.

## The family's emblem

A family can have an **emblem**: a coat of arms, a mark, or a photograph
that stands for it.

- **The archive's own emblem** shows at the head of every page and above the
  family's name on the overview. Whoever looks after the site sets it, in
  *Administration → Archive → Emblem or coat of arms of the family*.
- **Each family** can have one too. It shows beside the union on the
  family's pages, and on the couple's knot in the tree.

**To give a family its emblem** (you need to be allowed to edit):

1. Open the page of one of the couple and click **Edit**.
2. In the **Family** tab, on the union, click **Emblem or coat of arms**.
3. Either choose a picture already attached to the family, or, under **Add a
   picture**, choose the file and press **Add it and use it**.
4. Press **Save**. **No emblem** takes it away again.

A coat of arms is shown whole, never cut to fit. Who can see a family's
emblem is the same as who can see its people; the archive's emblem is shown
to every visitor.

## What the AFG remembers of you

The **AFG**, the Artificial family genealogist, is an assistant that reads
the family's archive and answers questions about it. Whoever looks after the
site decides which accounts may talk to it.

It keeps a memory of each person who talks to it, its *brain*: a few facts
it chose to remember about you, and your last exchanges with it. They help
it pick up where you left off. To see them, open **My account**, at the top
right, then **Your AFG's brain**.

- **Forget** beside a fact or an exchange removes it. The AFG will no longer
  be told it.
- **Forget everything…** removes all of it, once you tick the box.

Nobody else sees your brain on the site, not even an administrator.

## Correcting something

You need to be signed in, and your account needs permission to edit — if it
does not, you will see the record but no **Edit** links, and whoever set this
up can change that.

1. Open the person's page.
2. Click the **Edit** link for the part you want to change. The name, the
   birth, the death, the family links and the attached files each have their
   own editor, so you are never confronted with the whole record at once.
3. Change what is wrong. Leave everything else alone.
4. Press **Save**.

Places and sources are not typed in by hand. Where a field asks for one, it
offers the ones already in the archive as you type — start typing `Kra` and
"Kraków" appears; choose it from the list. If what you want is not there yet,
somebody has to add the place itself first. Typing a name that matches nothing
is refused with a message rather than saved as a mistake.

**If two of you edit the same person at once**, the second person to press Save
is shown both versions side by side and asked which should stand. Nobody's work
is silently thrown away.

**If a save fails**, read the message: it says what stopped it and — this is
the important part — whether anything was changed. "Nothing was written and the
existing data is untouched" means exactly that.

## Adding a photograph

1. Open the person's page and go to the **Media** tab, or use the
   **Attach and detach documents** link on the Record tab.
2. Under **Upload a file**, choose the file on your computer.
3. Give it a **caption** — "Maria and Jan, wedding, 1938" is worth ten times
   "scan 4". The caption is what everybody else will see under it.
4. Press the upload button.

Up to 50 MB per file. Photographs from a phone are usually 2–5 MB and a
scanned PDF can be much larger; a file over the limit is refused as soon as
you choose it, with a line beside it that says so, and nothing is sent.
Photographs, scans and PDFs are all accepted.

The file goes **into the archive itself**, not into a folder somewhere that
might get lost. When the archive is backed up, your photograph is in the
backup.

To make a photograph the picture that stands for someone, use **Choose
picture** on their record. You can click the part of the picture that should
stay in frame — the small square beside a name is square and most photographs
are not.

## The little "confidence" marker

Many facts carry a number or a small marker labelled **Confidence**. It is the
archive's way of saying *how sure anybody is* about that particular fact.

- A **high** confidence means somebody read it off a birth certificate or a
  parish register.
- A **low** one means it was inferred, remembered, or copied from a tree
  somebody else assembled quickly.

It matters because a date read off a certificate and a date somebody guessed
look identical once they are both typed into a box. This is the archive
refusing to let them look identical.

If everything in the tree shows the same confidence, that is the mark of a
bulk import: a starting value nobody has revisited. Lowering or raising one as
you check it against a document is one of the most useful things you can do
here.

## Reading it in your own language

The language name in the top bar opens the list. Fourteen languages are
complete: English, Polish, German, French, Spanish, Italian, Portuguese,
Russian, Arabic, Hebrew, Japanese, Chinese, Swahili and Lingala. Arabic and
Hebrew lay the whole page out right to left.

The choice is remembered on your own computer and changes nothing for anybody
else. Names, places and captions are shown exactly as they were entered —
choosing German does not translate great-grandmother's name.

## Things worth knowing

**You cannot break this.** Every change is recorded with your name against it,
nothing is overwritten without being kept, and the whole archive is backed up
every night and checked once a week. The worst you can do is enter something
wrong, and somebody can see what you did and put it right.

**There is no "delete everything" button** anywhere you can reach.

**If a page says something is withheld**, that is the privacy rule working. A
living person's details are not shown to people who should not see them, and
the page says so plainly rather than pretending.

**If something looks broken** — a photograph that will not open, a page that
will not load — tell whoever set this up, and say what you were doing at the
time. There is a technician's version of this document at
[docs/OPERATOR.md](OPERATOR.md); the first thing in it is how they check
whether the machine is healthy.
