## Context

On 11 November 2026, looking at ax-genealogy.com's sign-in page, the
operator asked for what it lacks most: a forgotten password. For that the
site must send mail — through an SMTP relay such as Gmail, set in the
configuration and in the administration — and once it can, it must also
write when an account is made for someone, nicely laid out and in the
language the account was made in. They also asked that members be told by
mail of activity on the records they follow and of new notes.

Until now the product sent nothing. ADR 0012 §2 refused an address typed at
registration, because a confirmed address would count for linking a Google
sign-in to an account, and that is how a stranger's sign-in lands in a
visitor's account. An adversarial reading of a first draft of this ADR found
that hole again, and others: tokens nothing cancels, links mail scanners
spend, a forgotten-password page whose timing names accounts, mail a
signed-out reader cannot follow past the sign-in, Gmail's daily cap, and an
administration never told that mail stopped. This ADR is the first part: the
relay, the forgotten password, the welcome. Member-typed addresses and
notifications need a trust model and a journal cursor of their own, and come
next, in another ADR.

## Decision

### 1. One relay, from the administration or the configuration

The relay is a mailbox made for the site — for Gmail, a Google account of its
own, never a person's: an app password reads that account's mail as well as
sending it. Three kinds are described in docs/OPERATOR.md: Gmail (587 with
STARTTLS or 465 with TLS, an app password, which needs 2-Step
Verification), a provider (its host, port and a sender it has verified), and
a relay on this machine (127.0.0.1 or ::1, port 25, no security, no
credentials — it needs no opening of the service's sandbox and keeps mail
queued across restarts).

`Site.mail` holds it, set in Connectivity, in a section of its own: host,
port, security, user name, password, sender address and name, an optional
Reply-To, the language for mail when an account has none, and a daily
ceiling. The password is shown as set or unset, never sent back, and can be
cleared; the section says, beside it, where it lives (family.acl and every
backup) and that it reads the mailbox. It goes only to the relay it was
given for: a new host, port, security or user name needs it typed again, or
a stolen session could point the relay at a server of its own and press the
test. With Gmail the section warns when the
sender is not the user name: Gmail sends only as the account or an alias it
has verified. The form refuses 465 with STARTTLS and 587 with TLS.

The configuration can set the relay instead: `AXGF_CMS_SMTP_URL`, read by
the site's own parser — `smtp://` means STARTTLS required, never plain;
`smtps://` means TLS; `?security=none` is accepted for a loopback host only;
user name and password percent-encoded, spaces dropped from the password —
and `AXGF_CMS_MAIL_FROM` (`Name <address>`), ignored without the URL. Set,
they win, and the section shows host and sender without the password; what
the section adds to any relay — the reply address, the language, the
ceiling — is still set there. A
value that does not parse leaves mail off, is logged at start and shown in
the section; it never stops the service. Either takes effect after
`systemctl restart axgf-cms`.

The client is `lettre`'s blocking SMTP transport, over rustls, ring and
webpki-roots, the TLS stack already in the tree, run off the async workers.
The installed unit refuses outgoing connections; docs/OPERATOR.md gives the
drop-in (`IPAddressAllow=any`), as for the sign-in services.

### 2. Sent beside the request, and watched

A handler never waits on SMTP, except the section's test. It hands a message
whole — recipient, subject, a text part and an HTML part — to the mailer,
which sends it in a task of its own, two at a time, each wait on the relay
bounded to thirty seconds. Only a temporary failure is tried again, after
one and five minutes (a 4xx reply, a wait past its bound, a refused or cut
connection, a name that did not resolve), and not once mail is paused. An authentication
refusal (530, 534, 535) pauses the mailer until the relay is saved again or
the test succeeds; a recipient refused for good (550 5.1.x) drops that
message, and the accounts page shows the refusal on the account; Gmail's
daily limit (550 5.4.5) pauses the mailer until the next UTC day, a test
that gets through, or another relay. A message asked for during a pause, or
past the ceiling below, is not kept for later: it is not sent, the limits of
§5 it was counted against are given back, and the person asks again.

The mailer keeps a daily ceiling of recipients (300 by default, set in the
section), counted in `family.mailstate` beside the bundle, so a restart does
not reset it. Past it nothing is sent, and the section and the dashboard say
so. The same file keeps the last success and the last failure apart, the
counts accepted and refused over a day, the pause and its reason, and each
account's last message and outcome, which the accounts page shows. An
authentication refusal, or three failures in a row, puts a warning on the
dashboard and an item on the Infrastructure checks, pointing at the section.
Messages waiting at a restart are not kept: the service log says how many,
and the person asks again — for a welcome, the accounts page sends it again.

No log line holds a token, a link, a subject, a body or what was typed into
the forgotten-password form: a message is logged by kind and account, with
the address masked and addresses removed from the relay's reply.

The test sends directly, outside the queue, within fifteen seconds — each
wait on the relay bounded to ten, so its real answer comes back — to an
address typed in its own field (the administrator's own by default), and
says what came back: "accepted by smtp.gmail.com: 250 …" — accepted, not yet
delivered — or a plain reason: the service's sandbox refused the connection
(the drop-in), the relay refused the credentials (for Gmail, an app
password), or TLS failed (465 goes with TLS, 587 with STARTTLS).

### 3. One layout, written in Rust and in one template

Every message is the same shape: a title, a greeting, a few paragraphs, at
most one button with its link written out beneath, small print, a footer
with the family's name and the site's address. `src/mail_messages.rs` builds
the shape in the recipient's language with the catalogue's keys, isolating
names in Arabic and Hebrew; `templates/mail.html` lays it out with tables
and inline styles, in the colours of the recipient's theme read from
`tokens.css`, with the logo as an inline PNG and `dir="rtl"` where it
belongs. The text part is written from the same shape in Rust. Messages are
rendered with a context of their own, never through the request's chrome,
whose language would not be the recipient's. Every key is in all fourteen
catalogues, and a test asks each language for each.

The language: the account's interface language if the member chose one;
else the language the account was made in (`User.mail_language`, recorded at
every creation through a page — the language the administrator picks on the
form, defaulting to theirs, or the visitor's for an account made through a
service or the registration form; not for one made with `--create-admin`);
else, for a forgotten password, the language of the request; else the
language of the member's last sign-in (recorded at each, by password,
through a service or by a mailed link); else the section's language for
mail; English last.

Every message says `Auto-Submitted: auto-generated`, has a Message-ID of
the sender's domain, and carries the section's Reply-To when one is set. A
test's footer says it was sent from the mail settings, not that an account
holds the address.

### 4. Links that hold and need nothing stored

A link is good only for what it was made for, and stops working on its own
when what it was made for has changed. A token is the account id, a purpose
and an expiry, with two HMAC-SHA256 under a key of its own (`family.mailkey`,
mode 600 beside the bundle, never in a backup): one over those three, which
says the token was made here; one over them and digests of the account's
password hash, role, status and address, a count of the changes that end
its links for good (`User.link_epoch`, raised by a disabling, a new role or
a new address) — and, for a welcome, of its last sign-in — which says
nothing has changed since. Setting a password by any way, a new address, a
new role, a disabled account or, for a welcome, a first sign-in each end
every link the account had, and undoing the change does not bring them
back; nothing else is stored, and nothing needs cleaning up. Nothing is written when a link is made, so asking for one takes
no lock another request could notice.

A link opens a page and spends nothing; only its form spends it. A token
that was never made here, posted to that form, counts as one failure of the
password kind in the jail; a token that was good once and is not any more is
answered "this link is no longer good", with no charge. A jail term blocks
neither the forgotten-password page nor a good link.

### 5. The forgotten password

"Forgot your password?" on the sign-in page — and on its 401 variant — leads
to `/mail/forgot`: a user name or an address. The answer is the same page
whatever was typed, and it comes before anything is looked up: the lookup,
the link and the message happen in the background, so neither the page nor
its timing says whether an account exists. Without a relay or a public
address the page says that an administrator sets passwords.

A link goes only to an account that is active, has a password already, has
an address it may trust (`User.email`: an administrator typed it or a
service verified it), and whose role may use a password. An address two
accounts share names neither. An account that signs in only through a service
receives instead a message naming that service, and nothing that adds a
password. Requests are counted per client (eight in a quarter of an hour),
per account (three messages an hour, six a day) and per recipient address
(one message with a link a quarter of an hour, five a day); past them the
page is the same and nothing is sent.

The link, `/mail/set/<token>`, is good for an hour. Its form asks for the
new password twice, twelve characters at least. The token is checked before
the password is hashed, the hash is computed on the bounded path, and the
token is checked again, against the account as it then is, in the same
write that stores the hash — so two posts of one link cannot both win. Then
every session of the account ends, its failed sign-ins are cleared, this
browser is signed in, and the account's address is told the password was
changed. The pages under `/mail/` are `no-store`, not in the access log,
and say `Referrer-Policy: strict-origin`: under the site's `same-origin`
their own stylesheets would carry the token, in their Referer, into the
proxy's log. The journal names `/mail/set/…` without its token.

An administrator who sets a member's password from the accounts page sends
the member the same "your password was changed" message.

### 6. The welcome

An administrator making an account may give its address and the language of
its welcome, in a closed part of the form. With a relay, a public address
and a role that may use a password, the welcome gives the user name and a
link to choose a password — seven days, one for an administrator's account,
ending at the first sign-in — the same page as the reset, titled as a
welcome; no password is shown on the page, and none ever travels by mail. A
role that may not use a password is welcomed with the services to sign in
with, and no password is shown either: it could not be used. When no welcome
goes — no relay, no public address, a message to that address a moment ago,
mail held back — the page shows the generated password once, as before, and
says that nothing was sent. The accounts page can send the welcome again: to
an account that has never signed in, a new welcome; to one that has, a reset
link.

An account made through a sign-in service is welcomed at the address the
service verified, in the visitor's language, with the service to sign in
with. An account made with the registration form gives no address, as ADR
0012 §2 still has it, and receives nothing.

### 7. Links that survive the sign-in

A signed-out reader sent to the sign-in by a door, by `/account` or by a
mail link comes back where they were going: the redirect carries
`next=<path>`, checked to be a path of this site, and a sign-in by password
or through a service returns there. Without one, a reader goes to the river
and a writer to the administration, as a service's sign-in already did.

### 8. The door

The visitor pages live under `/mail/`, outside every part of the site, so a
closed site still serves them. The shipped nginx file gives them a location
of their own, counted with the sign-in's posts and kept out of its access
log; a proxy written by hand must add it.

## Consequences

- A forgotten password no longer needs an administrator, once a relay and a
  public address are set.
- An account an administrator makes can be told it exists, in its language,
  without a password ever travelling by mail.
- Links need nothing stored and outlive a restart; they die with any change
  to what they were made for.
- Mail leaves the site: a user name, a family's name and the site's address
  go to the relay and to the member's mailbox.
- A relay password set in the administration lives in `family.acl` and its
  backups; one set in the configuration does not.
- A rollback to 1.16.0 forgets the mail settings' new fields on its next
  write of the accounts, and links made before it stop working.
